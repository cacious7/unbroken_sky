# The Unbroken Sky

## The hand behind the wall

The other child could hear birds.

Tavo knew this because she told him every morning, although he had never heard one himself. There was a wall between them. Somewhere inside the wall ran a narrow copper strip that grew warm when they pressed their hands against it.

“Perhaps it's the pipes,” he said.

“Pipes don't quarrel over fruit.”

He considered this. He had forgotten what fruit tasted like, but he remembered the quarrelling.

“Is there sky where you are?”

“There must be,” she said. “Birds don't carry it with them.”

Sometimes he saw what she saw: a thin window, a foot in a yellow sandal, sunlight crossing a bowl. Sometimes he remembered standing beside her even though they had never occupied the same room. The keepers said this meant the work was going well.

That morning, a woman opened his door.

She was not wearing her official sash. She took his wrist and wrapped it in cloth before lifting him. Beyond her shoulder he saw the second door. It remained closed.

“The girl,” he whispered.

The woman's hand tightened.

There were footsteps somewhere above them.

“I'll bring her back,” he told the wall.

He did not know whether the other child heard.

## The well

Seventeen years later, Tavo had forgotten the room but still disliked sleeping with the door shut.

His sister considered this one of his less troublesome habits.

That morning Sena sat beside a brazier, arguing with a copper bird. Its wings were meant to move when heat rose beneath them. One wing moved. The other refused.

“The balance is wrong,” Tavo said.

“Good morning to you too.”

“Make that one lighter.”

“Touch it and I'll make your bag heavier.”

He left her to it and went to the well. Morning light touched the red slopes above the settlement. Already the ground was warming. People would work until the sun drove them beneath the shade cloth, then return when the stones stopped burning their feet. Tavo knew which paths cooled first, where a cart could be rested, which dry-looking gullies hid a little moisture. Outsiders called this barren country. Outsiders often arrived without enough water.

The pump handle caught halfway down.

He took out the worn pin and fetched a new one from the basket. His mother had taught him to repair what was broken before polishing what people could see.

The first stroke brought clear water.

The second brought black grit.

On the third, something inside the pipe said, “Present.”

Tavo let go.

Sena looked up. “Have you broken breakfast?”

Their mother crossed the yard so quickly that her stool fell behind her. Mera seized his wrist before he could touch the water.

“Wash your hand.”

“I haven't—”

“Not in that.”

Black silt swirled in the bowl. For a moment three old cuts on his wrist seemed darker than the rest of his skin. Mera covered them with her fingers.

“What is it?” he asked.

She stared at the pipe.

“Something from below.”

Sena set the bird aside. Neither child made another joke.

At the cistern, Daru had already stopped distribution. Twelve clean jars remained beneath a locked awning. Beyond the settlement gate, refugees stood with their containers. They had been arriving for weeks, bringing accounts of the ruined river capital, strange sicknesses, and roads no longer safe after dark.

A woman in a patched blue wrap stepped out of the queue.

“I have two people who cannot swallow,” she said. “I need water before permission.”

Daru rubbed his eyes. Tavo had known him since they were small enough to steal fruit through the same hole in a fence. Now he wore a militia captain's belt and slept beside a list of households.

“These are the last clean reserves,” Daru said.

“Then we both have a problem.”

Her name was Nali. She had been a healer in the capital. Tavo heard the city in her careful speech and felt an unreasonable irritation. People from the river courts had always sounded as though they were explaining things to a slow child.

Then one of her patients tried to stand and fell against the gate.

“Two jars now,” Tavo said.

Daru turned to him.

“The old spring has a blocked settling channel. They have workers. We have pipe. Open it together.”

“And if it fails?”

“We still have ten jars.”

Nali looked at him, then at Daru. “One keeper from each side counts what comes through.”

They argued over the measuring bowl before agreeing. The argument reassured Tavo more than gratitude would have. By noon, a little brown water was settling behind the gate. It was not enough, and would need treating, but the two patients had drunk.

The main supply depended on an old channel controlled at Reed Gate, a fortress downstream. Tavo had delivered messages there before. Someone had to go.

Mera examined a seal Nali had brought from another group of travellers.

“Eren is alive,” she said.

“You know the keeper?” Tavo asked.

“I know his office.”

She would say nothing more until they were home. Even then, she answered his questions by packing his bag.

“Don't answer a voice from water. Don't promise anything inside a sealed chamber.”

“Are you telling me how to travel, or what you've done?”

Her hands stopped.

Sena looked between them.

“When you were seven,” Mera said, “I brought you home sick. There are things about that sickness I haven't told you.”

“Tell me now.”

“I need to fetch something first. Something I should have given you years ago. When you return, you will have it.”

Tavo waited. She resumed packing.

He left angry.

At the gate, Sena pushed the copper bird into his hand.

“Test it somewhere with wind.”

“Does it work?”

“One side does. Try not to fall towards the other.”

## A borrowed face

The Ash Road followed an escarpment where the air rose fiercely in the afternoon. Tavo stayed beneath the rock shelves until he reached an abandoned way station.

Something moved inside it.

At first he thought a worker was repairing the roof. Then the figure turned, and he saw the empty space beneath its copper faceplate.

The old maintenance shell carried a tool arm. It had once cleaned channels and lifted sluice pins. Now it scraped the same line across the wall, over and over.

A merchant crouched behind an overturned cart.

“I told it I owned the road,” the man whispered. “It stopped to consider that.”

“For how long?”

“Not long enough.”

The shell turned towards Tavo's footsteps. He had time to see its arm draw back before the blade struck the stone where he had stood. He dodged behind a pillar and hit its knee joint with his repair hammer. Copper rang. The shell lurched. Its next blow caught a wheel of the cart, trapping the tool arm long enough for him to tear out the exposed connector beneath its neck.

It folded to the ground.

His hands shook afterwards. He hid them by righting the merchant's cart.

Nali found him there. She was travelling to the fortress with medicine, carrying more than he thought sensible and refusing to let him take any of it until she had inspected his bleeding sleeve.

“Not my blood,” he said.

“Then somebody else needs that cloth.”

They walked together.

Near a dry culvert, his mother called his name.

She stood on the far side wearing the scarf Sena had repaired last season. Her clothes dripped into dust that did not darken.

Tavo stopped so abruptly that Nali struck his shoulder.

“Mera?”

“Come help your mother.”

He took one step.

Nali caught his bag. In a shallow pool beneath the culvert, the woman's reflection was speaking out of time with her mouth.

“Ask her something,” Nali whispered.

“What?”

“Something difficult.”

Tavo swallowed.

“What happened when I was seven?”

The woman smiled.

“What happened when I was seven?” she repeated, in his voice.

He moved before he understood he was moving. Water struck the place where he had stood. The borrowed face lengthened, pulling apart like wet cloth. Nali dragged him towards the bank while he swung the hammer at a copper hook set into the culvert wall.

The first blow bent it. The second tore it loose.

The figure collapsed into a shallow spill.

Neither traveller spoke for a while.

“What was that?” he asked at last.

“I don't know.”

“You knew how to test it.”

“My patients sometimes call for people who aren't there. You listen before answering.”

Tavo picked up the hook. An official mark had been stamped into it.

Someone had built a place for that thing to hold on.

## Reed Gate

The fortress stood above a reservoir crowded with empty carts. An admission order was still nailed to its gate. Only certified households had been allowed inside when the catastrophe came.

A woman on the wall told them to move away from the spillway.

Tavo asked for the release wheel that controlled the upland channel.

“So does everyone downstream,” she said.

Nali stepped forward. “Veya. Open the clinic.”

The woman's expression changed. A ladder came down.

Inside, Veya worked with her sleeves tied back and a brace supporting an injured leg. Tavo recognised her name only after seeing the royal signet hanging from a chain at her throat. She was an engineer of the ruling family. He had expected servants, clean hands and an explanation of why everyone else must wait.

Instead she showed him a cracked gate.

“Turn the wheel now and the upper reservoir empties into those rooms.”

He could hear people beyond the wall.

“Then show me the repair.”

They spent the afternoon draining a side channel and fitting a brace. Sena's new key matched the mechanism. Tavo tried not to imagine her expression when he told her.

Behind a grille below the gate, a man whispered, “Present.”

Tavo's hand slipped.

Nali went down first. The keeper lay with his wrist fastened to a copper band. His eyes were clear, although his skin had the dry fragility of old paper.

“Seven came back,” he said when Tavo approached.

“My name is Tavo.”

“Of course she gave you one.”

The room tilted.

He was standing outside the grille. He was kneeling inside it. He could feel a bowl against his knees and the rough stone under someone else's palm.

He caught the wall.

“You know my mother.”

Eren closed his eyes.

“I want to tell you I only counted them.”

“Counted whom?” Nali asked.

“I put numbers where their names had been.”

Above them, something cracked.

Eren seized Tavo's sleeve. “Archive courtyard. Blue drain tile. I kept the transfer slips.”

Nali was already examining the fastening.

“Wait,” she told him. “I can—”

The channel behind Eren lit beneath the stone. Water hammered the grille. Tavo reached for him, but Nali pulled him towards the stairs as the room filled.

“Don't let them make you the answer again,” Eren said.

A section of wall tore open.

For an instant Tavo hung between the broken stair and the river. He saw Nali's reaching hand, the fortress wall, his own bag turning beneath him. The copper bird fell out of his pocket.

He tried to catch it.

His fingers opened into feathers.

There was no time to be astonished. He struck the air badly, felt one wing buckle, and dropped towards the rocks.

Left, a voice said inside him. The air comes up there.

He turned.

The wind caught him so suddenly that fear became something vast and weightless. The fortress fell away. Every ledge was visible. He could feel the space above the reservoir as though it had become another part of his body.

Then the voice told him to land, and he obeyed.

He found himself human on a narrow platform, bleeding from both hands. His bag had caught against a broken bracket below him. He hauled it up by the strap; the copper bird was tangled in it, one wing bent.

Nali climbed towards him. She stopped a few paces away.

“Tell me couriers do that,” he said.

“Not the ones I've paid.”

A hyena emerged from the stair shadow. Before he could reach for his hammer, its body changed. A woman stood where it had been.

“You nearly landed wrong,” she said.

“Who are you?”

“Kesi.” She looked at his wrist. “You asked me that before.”

Veya reached the platform with a weapon drawn.

“The woman who broke the renewal chamber.”

“The woman who opened it,” Kesi replied.

“My city heard the difference for one breath.”

Nali stepped between them. “There are injured people below us. The platform is breaking. Put it away.”

Another party arrived before the quarrel could resume. Its oldest member helped hold the new brace while Tavo seated the pin. Only afterwards did Tavo recognise the minister's seal on his chest.

Orun, senior minister of the House of Continuance, had come with relief workers. His son Lio went straight to the clinic.

With the bypass secure, they turned the wheel. Water moved through the old upland channel. Tavo watched until the flow ran clear.

Home had water again.

Then Orun saw his wrist.

“Mera has been hiding more than records.”

Tavo was too tired to be polite.

“Everyone knows my mother except me.”

Before sunset, a messenger reached the fortress. Mera had confessed to supplying the attackers with plans of the renewal chamber. Daru had detained her before frightened neighbours could do worse. River commanders were demanding her surrender. Sena was coming under his protection to seek a hearing at the capital's relief court.

Tavo looked at the clean water leaving the gate.

His errand was finished. Nothing he understood was safe.

## What remained of the city

They travelled towards the capital with the relief party. Kesi stayed at its edge, never close enough for Veya to touch her.

At camp, Lio tried to repair a shoe with a needle too large for the leather.

“My father says I should learn something useful.”

“How long has he been saying that?” Nali asked.

“Ten years.”

“At some point it becomes your fault.”

Tavo laughed before he remembered he was afraid. Lio laughed too, and cut his finger. Nali took the shoe away.

That evening she told Tavo her father had maintained the public baths. He had collected treatment tokens for people who could not afford to miss work to obtain them.

“He believed a city that washed strangers couldn't be wholly cruel.”

“Was he wrong?”

“About the baths? No.”

Beyond the camp, the capital's towers stood dark against the last light. Tavo had delivered packages there once. He remembered fruit piled in shaded markets, the smell of hot oil, children chasing each other through running water. He had resented the extravagance and wanted to stay.

At the ruined archive courtyard the next morning, he found Sena waiting beneath an awning. She hugged him hard, then pushed him back.

“You flew?”

“I fell in an unusual direction.”

“Show me.”

“After the hearing,” Daru said.

Mera sat in a screened room nearby. A water bowl and food had been provided. The sight of the guards outside it made Tavo angry; the sight of her unharmed made him ashamed of his relief.

The hearing gathered people who did not agree that anyone present had the right to judge. Daru insisted on witnesses from the uplands. Nali spoke for the refugees. Veya laid out the royal order. Orun suspended it within the courtyard until evidence could be heard, although he admitted he could not promise every commander would obey.

Mera confessed without looking at Tavo.

“I gave Kesi the route into the chamber. I believed the peripheral channels could take the load when the prisoners were disconnected.”

“Thousands died,” Veya said.

“Yes.”

“Did you know the channels were sealed?” Orun asked.

“Not then.”

Kesi stepped out from the awning's edge.

“I knew before I opened the centre.”

For a moment nobody moved.

“The guards were returning,” she said. “They were going to complete the renewal. The prisoners were still alive inside.”

“So you decided for every household along the river,” Daru said.

“Others had been deciding for those prisoners all their lives.”

“That doesn't answer me.”

“No,” Kesi said. “It doesn't.”

Tavo had expected denial, an excuse, a revelation that made her innocent. Her answer frightened him more.

He asked for the archive to be opened.

Before entering it, he went to his mother.

“Did you steal me?”

She sat very still.

“I took you out of a room no child should enter.”

“That isn't the same answer.”

“No.”

Outside, someone was calling for more bandages. He waited for the voice to stop.

“Your mother was called Ilen,” Mera said. “She petitioned for your return.”

He had not known he was waiting to hear a name until it was given to him.

“Where is she?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you read the petition?”

“Yes.”

“Then you know something.”

Mera opened a small packet. Inside was a folded letter, softened along its creases.

“I should have given you this before you had to ask.”

He could not bring himself to open it.

“Why did you take me?”

Her mouth trembled. He wanted an answer large enough to contain the life they had lived together.

“You were nearest the service door.”

He looked away.

“I went back three years later for Sena,” she said. “I didn't bring everyone.”

He remembered her tying ropes around him when they repaired deep wells, checking every knot twice.

“You taught me not to leave anyone down there.”

“I know.”

## The other side

The archive smelled of wet stone and old soot. Records had been stored above the flood line, but channels ran through the lower walls. Tavo recognised the warmth beneath his hand before he recognised the copper.

He pressed his palm against it.

The room vanished.

He was seven, and someone on the other side of the wall could hear birds.

This time he saw the woman's face when she entered. Mera was younger, frightened, trying to carry him and hold a door at once. He saw another angle too: a window high above a bowl, a small hand against the copper, a door that remained closed.

“I'll bring her back,” his younger voice said.

Tavo pulled away.

Kesi stood at the archive doorway.

“It was you.”

She nodded.

“I left you.”

“You were seven.”

“I said I would come back.”

“You were seven.”

He sat on the floor because his legs would no longer hold him.

For seventeen years he had believed there was something missing inside his childhood. Now he could see someone had been left in the space.

Kesi waited until he could breathe normally.

“Sometimes you hear me. Sometimes you hear an old trace. Don't trust a memory just because it hurts.”

“Are there others?”

“Yes.”

He wanted to ask how many. She turned away before he did.

Together they uncovered the blue drain tile. Eren's transfer slips were sealed beneath it. The records named households, exemptions, deliveries and shortened contributions. Mera's register, brought from Red Hollow, completed their meaning.

The first covenant had been made after a rupture beneath the river exposed a place where the living world no longer held its boundary securely. The ward required renewal, but no household was meant to give more than it could recover. Several stations had shared the work. Representatives could be replaced. Agreements could expire.

Over generations, temporary powers had become inherited offices. Wealthy households purchased exemptions. Officials began signing for people they had never met. Losses in distant districts were entered as local failures: disease, poor harvests, ancestral displeasure.

When the remaining households could no longer carry the demands, keepers began recruiting children born with a rare capacity to change form. Connected together, they could receive far greater loads.

Tavo found his number.

Then Sena's.

He closed the register so hard that dust fell from the beam above him.

In the clinic, Nali laid a free treatment token beside an outlying village's death record. The same stamp marked both. Veya found her own signature on an expansion order.

“I was told these channels carried furnace heat,” she said.

She traced a line continuing beneath the boundary on the diagram.

“What did you think the increase would do?” Tavo asked.

“Warm the children's ward.”

Nali turned the treatment token over in her fingers. Her father's work had saved people. She would not pretend otherwise. Neither would she let that work answer for the people whose lives had subsidised it.

Tavo wanted every record read aloud at once. Nali stopped him when he reached the list of surviving recruits.

“Those names lead to homes.”

“People need to know.”

“They need to know what was done. That doesn't give them Sena's address.”

He looked at his sister across the courtyard. She was repairing a cracked pot handle with wire, unaware that strangers had nearly been given her childhood.

They prepared a protected account instead. Mera's office remained named. So did the officials whose signatures could be verified. Survivor identities were withheld unless their owners chose to speak.

The hearing grew bitter. A woman whose husband had died accused Nali of inventing an excuse for the attackers. An old clerk insisted the accounts had always been lawful. Another attendant brought forward a record she had kept despite instructions to destroy it.

No single reading made the courtyard agree.

But by evening there were copies, and witnesses to what had been shown.

## The letter

Sena found Tavo behind the clinic.

“Were you going to tell me?” she asked.

He did not pretend to misunderstand.

“I was going to find out more first.”

“About my life.”

“Our lives.”

“That isn't the same thing.”

She put her wrist beside his. The three cuts matched, although hers were smaller.

“I remember hands that aren't mine,” she said. “I thought everybody did.”

“I thought mine were dreams.”

“You never asked about mine.”

Kesi explained what remained of the connection. Tavo and Sena had escaped years before the renewal. Five recruits remained inside, all grown now. Kesi had opened the chamber for them. Only she and one other had survived. That survivor was far downriver; sometimes she could still reach them. The dead left fragments, but no intact person to rescue.

Tavo asked whether the connection could be cut.

“Yes,” Kesi said. “We'd lose what only it carries. Borrowed senses. Shapes. Memories that never belonged to our own bodies.”

Sena was quiet for a long time.

“Then I want to know what choosing that would involve.”

Later, Kesi offered to show Tavo how to cross into her form. She asked before touching his wrist. The question itself made him hesitate; permission had rarely figured in anyone's explanation of what he was.

He agreed.

The hyena's world reached him in layers. Dust, old food, warm metal, fear-sweat on a discarded cloth. He followed a trail of machine oil through a low service passage beneath the archive and found a maintenance chamber nobody had entered since the flood.

When he emerged, Kesi was waiting.

“That shape goes with me if we separate,” she said.

“I know.”

“You say that like you've never borrowed a bowl you liked.”

For the first time, he heard what her laugh might have sounded like without the prison in it.

That night he read Ilen's letter.

She had written that he liked bitter fruit if it was cut small, although he always denied it. She asked for his shoes to be sent because his feet would be growing. She asked that he not sleep alone during rain. There had been a frightening noise in their roof.

Tell him I fixed it.

He read the sentence again.

On the reverse was Mera's handwriting.

Household verified. Collection continues.

He carried the letter to her room.

“She was trying to bring me home.”

“Yes.”

“And you used it to keep collecting from them.”

“Yes.”

He wanted her to give him something he could reject. A defence would have let him be angry without hearing anything else. She offered none.

“When did you decide it was wrong?”

“I knew parts of it were wrong long before I left.”

“Parts?”

“I told myself I was preventing worse mistakes. That somebody careful had to remain. Then I learned how to stop looking at what careful meant.”

He remembered fevers through which she had stayed awake beside him. He remembered her irritation, her cooking, the way she counted his tools before a journey so he would not leave anything useful behind.

None of those memories disappeared.

“You are my mother,” he said at last. “You will still answer for it.”

She bowed her head.

He left before she could thank him.

## Enough for ours

Orun was serving grain to people who had scratched out his order's emblem above the awning.

Lio asked whether they should be moved to another queue.

“They came for food,” Orun said.

Tavo waited until the pots were empty before approaching him.

The minister took him to a lower stair where dark water had risen past yesterday's mark.

“The wound was real before anybody made a tax of it,” Orun said. “It will remain real after we have finished accusing one another.”

He showed Tavo a diagram of the central station. A keeper sat within it, surrounded by lines that gradually obscured the figure.

“You think I can replace them.”

“I think you may be able to hold the centre without the provincial levy.”

“For how long?”

“I don't know.”

Tavo examined the later drawings. The keeper's own mark had disappeared into the writing around him.

“What happened to the others who sat there?”

“They stopped distinguishing their wishes from the demands made through them.”

“And you want me to agree.”

“I want the river to stop taking people.”

“Will you accept no?”

Orun looked towards the rising water. His hesitation was answer enough.

Lio, listening from the stair, offered to take a station beside Tavo.

Orun closed his eyes. For an instant Tavo saw how much easier the offer would have been if it belonged to somebody else's son.

“I believe you mean it,” Tavo told Lio. “You still can't answer for me.”

When he returned to the courtyard, Daru had loaded grain onto two carts. He and Veya had reached an agreement: upland guards would protect the gate crews in exchange for priority water to Red Hollow.

“What happens downstream?” Tavo asked.

Daru checked the rope securing a sack.

“I counted the beds at home. The people waiting in them know my name.”

“There are people on that channel too.”

“There were children behind the other doors when your mother took you. You still call her Mother.”

Tavo stepped towards him. Daru did not move.

“You think I've stopped hearing them?” Daru asked. “I hear every person I turn away. Then I count again.”

Nali arrived while neither man knew what to say next. She proposed opening the royal settling reserve and giving both communities control of the gate. Veya refused at first. Those tanks were held against emergencies.

Nali stared at the ruined city around them.

“What are you waiting for?”

They argued until the courtyard lights were lit. Daru would not promise his guards before seeing the reserve opened. Veya would not surrender the wheel to people who did not know how to maintain it. Refugees would not provide workers if guards could bar them from their own water.

At last they agreed to begin with actions small enough to verify. Veya opened the reserve before Daru assigned the first watch. Nali chose a keeper; the local workers chose another. Veya trained both. Neither could shut the main gate alone. Release times were written where people queued, including the hours when there would not be enough.

Daru stood beside Tavo afterwards.

“If it fails,” he said, “you come home with me and explain it.”

“I will.”

Daru took his arm.

“I mean that.”

“So do I.”

## The work before dawn

The chamber Tavo had found as a hyena contained the older relay plans.

Sena spread them beside Veya's diagrams. The first designers had expected stations to fail. Separate controls allowed the others to carry a reduced load long enough for repairs or evacuation. The system required labour, modest renewable offerings from willing adults, and much less consumption than the capital had grown used to.

It had been dismantled piece by piece because centralisation was easier to administer. Once control lay in one place, exemptions were easier to grant and harder to challenge.

“There isn't enough capacity for the upper gardens,” Veya said.

Nali studied the clinic allocation.

“The wards?”

“Basic care. Not every powered treatment.”

“What replaces them?”

“Some can use ordinary heat and daylight. Some cannot.”

Nobody celebrated the diagram.

But it was a plan they could inspect. It did not require Tavo to disappear inside a chair.

Orun examined it, raised two objections, and found one flaw in the proposed release order. Veya corrected it. Tavo had wanted him to oppose everything; accepting his useful criticism was unexpectedly difficult.

The remaining night became a chain of small tasks. Tavo carried a light wire across the courtyard in eagle form. He could not lift the brace that needed moving below; six people did that with ropes. As a hyena he found the old oil line beneath the archive. Sena replaced a cracked pin with metal taken from an ornamental fitting in Veya's abandoned rooms.

The royal engineer watched it go into the furnace.

“Was it important?” Sena asked.

“Very.”

Sena waited.

“Less than this,” Veya admitted.

Mera compared the names on the old agreements and identified which authorities had been wrongly transferred. Her knowledge was indispensable. Tavo hated that sometimes. He made himself ask the questions anyway.

Near dawn, Nali brought food to the workers. Kesi sat beside Tavo on a broken stair. Through the connection came a memory of red flowers along a riverbank, warm ground beneath bare feet, an afternoon with nothing urgent in it.

“Whose is that?” he asked.

“Mine.”

He tried to hold it more clearly.

“What happens to it if we succeed?”

“You can remember that I told you. You may not remember being there.”

He had accepted losing borrowed powers more easily than this small, peaceful thing.

Kesi looked down at her hands.

“I'm afraid too.”

By sunrise, three relay indicators answered one another across the diagram. The separate control points worked. The keepers rehearsed an evacuation. A pair of copies left the courtyard with messengers; more remained with attendants who did not belong to the same institution.

Far downriver, the fourth living survivor answered Kesi through the existing connection. They agreed to severance on the condition that no station could bind them again. Sena demanded the same condition. Veya incorporated it into the physical controls before anybody descended.

There was no triumph in finishing. There was a little less uncertainty.

## Beneath the river

The lower chamber was smaller than Tavo expected.

He had imagined something equal to the suffering it contained. Instead there were walls, channels, worn steps, and places where people had set down bowls. The cage door from his memories lay flat across a gap. Kesi's rescuers had used it as a bridge.

She stopped beside three empty floor stations.

Tavo let her remain there without asking for names she had not offered.

Orun showed them the seal where the peripheral channels had been closed.

“The renewal council approved this,” he said. “I sat on it.”

Veya examined the engineer's notation. It belonged to the expansion she had signed.

“I accepted a maintenance summary,” Orun continued. “The repair cost was low. I thought that meant the work was simple.”

Mera touched her old route plan.

“I gave Kesi a way out that was no longer there.”

“And I opened it knowing,” Kesi said.

Their responsibilities were different. Tavo could see why each of them wanted a simpler account.

Water moved behind the wall.

A shape rose in the central channel, gathering height until Mera stood before them.

“I can bring them home,” it said.

Its face changed to Daru's.

“Enough for ours.”

Then Nali's.

“No one left outside.”

Copper bands began tightening across the walkway. Tavo struck the nearest anchor while Veya worked at the manual shutoff. Water swept the floor. Orun pulled Mera clear of a closing band. Sena braced the relay line against the wall.

The shape watched Tavo with his own face.

“I will carry it.”

He swung the hammer. The first anchor broke.

The channel filled with a woman's voice he had never heard and somehow recognised.

“Tell him I fixed it.”

His arm fell.

For a moment there was only the letter. The shoes. The frightening roof. A mother who might have waited, might still be alive, might have spent seventeen years believing he had forgotten her.

The voice called again.

Kesi seized his shoulder.

It had used Mera's voice at the culvert. It had repeated his own question. It knew the letter because he had read it beside a channel. An exact voice could still be assembled from something stolen.

Tavo gripped the hammer.

“You heard a letter,” he said. “That's all.”

He broke the second anchor.

The thing offered him silence next. No borrowed grief. No voices. No one depending on him. He could rest.

He wanted it so badly that for a breath he hated everyone behind him.

Then Sena shouted that the relay line was slipping. She needed him to hold a clamp.

He went to her.

Together they secured it. He crossed the narrow bridge, struck the last anchor, and reached the shutoff. The shape folded into the channel. The chamber fell quiet enough for him to hear everyone breathing.

## The open hands

The central seat remained intact.

Orun stood beside it, exhausted.

“This will contain it tonight,” he said. “The other bindings can be released first. You would not take them with you.”

Tavo looked at the worn place where his hands would rest.

He could end the immediate terror. There would be no need to ask frightened communities whether they could keep an agreement. No need to wait while people contested allocations or lied about what they could contribute.

Everyone could ask him instead.

He remembered Daru counting the beds. He remembered Mera choosing the nearest door. He had spent the journey discovering how terrible decisions could become ordinary when the people making them believed there was no alternative.

Behind him, Sena checked the connection to the relays.

“The second station?” he asked.

“Ready.”

“The last survivor?”

“Kesi has their answer.”

“The clinic?”

Nali nodded. “We have moved the people who need the lower ward.”

He turned to Orun.

“We use the stations.”

The minister shut his eyes. When he opened them, he stepped away from the seat.

Veya read the order of operations. The independent keepers confirmed their controls. Kesi signalled her willingness. Sena did the same. The distant survivor's answer followed. Tavo released his own binding last.

For a moment the world held too much.

A child's hand against warm copper. A window he had never stood beneath. Red flowers near a river. Rain falling somewhere beyond the reach of his own body.

Then the borrowed impressions loosened.

He reached for the red flowers and found only the knowledge that he had liked them.

“Kesi?”

“I'm here.”

“Did we ever sit beside a river?”

“I did.”

He bowed his head.

Sena spoke his name. There was no doubled sound beneath it, no strange certainty about what she was feeling. He had to look at her face.

“Only you?” he asked.

“Only me.”

Above them, the great fountains stopped.

The new stations took the reduced load. One indicator flickered. A keeper called for an adjustment. Veya answered, then stopped herself and asked permission before changing the second control.

They waited.

The light steadied.

Nobody had destroyed the wound beneath the river. Nobody could promise it would never threaten another generation. They had made something that could be maintained, questioned and repaired without imprisoning a person at its centre.

When they reached the courtyard, Nali opened the clinic shutters. A bed had to be moved into daylight because its lamp no longer burned. Lio took one end. Orun took the other.

Tavo tried to become the hyena. Nothing answered.

He stood for a while, accepting the empty place where that sense had been.

Then he thought of the wind above Reed Gate. Feathers opened along his arms.

The eagle remained.

He rose once above the courtyard, circled the broken roof, and landed beside the workers moving a brace. Human hands were better for carrying it.

## What they carried home

In the weeks that followed, arguments returned before comfort did.

Water crews disagreed over shifts. A governor refused the new agreement and had to be left outside its benefits without being threatened with destruction. Some former citizens of the capital called the account an insult to their dead. Others brought records that complicated it further.

The clinics continued. Teachers found shade where their classrooms had been. Engineers repaired machines that did not depend on the covenant. The city had contained more human knowledge than its rulers' secret, and people began deciding what could be saved.

Veya trained gate operators. Nobody crowned her for it.

Orun remained a minister. Some attendants left the House; others argued for changes from within it. He returned to work among people who did not share his beliefs and learned, unevenly, not to mistake disagreement for ingratitude.

Kesi gave her account to the inquiry before travelling downriver to find the other survivor. Tavo walked with her to the road.

“I don't remember the flowers properly,” he said.

“I'll show you where they grow.”

This time a promise could be made between adults who knew what it meant.

Mera appeared before witnesses from the communities whose records she had administered. They could require restitution and bar her from keeping public accounts. Tavo did not ask to decide the verdict.

He visited after one hearing and found her sitting with food gone cold in her lap.

“Did you eat?” he asked.

“Sena brought something.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

For an instant she looked like the mother who used to find him avoiding meals before a journey. Then she took a mouthful.

He sat beside her. Neither treated the silence as forgiveness already granted.

At Red Hollow, Daru helped establish the joint watch. The refugee workers nominated their own deputy. Beside the settling spring, two keepers checked two measuring sticks, still unwilling to concede that the other's was straight.

Sena repaired the copper bird. Both wings turned now.

“You were right about the weight,” she told Tavo.

He opened his mouth.

“About one thing,” she added.

He carried Ilen's letter in a new wrapping. Somewhere beyond the roads he knew might be a person who remembered that household, that roof, the boy who denied liking bitter fruit. He intended to look. He no longer needed the search to make every part of his life explainable before he could live it.

On the morning he meant to leave, Nali walked with him to the gate.

“Take someone who'll tell you when you're wrong,” she said.

“I know several volunteers.”

She smiled and returned to the clinic.

Sena fastened the bird to his bag.

“Bring it back this time.”

“Still a test?”

“Still mine.”

He became the eagle and climbed above the red slopes. Below him, roofs caught the sun. The shared channel drew a dark line through the dry ground. Travellers moved between settlements that still disagreed about what had happened and what should happen next.

Beyond the gate, Daru was trying to push a cart out of a rut.

Tavo circled.

There were roads he wanted to see. There was enough sky to make him forget, briefly, how heavy a body could be.

He landed beside the cart and took the other handle.

Daru looked at him.

“All that sky.”

“You said you'd fetch me with a rope.”

“Tomorrow,” Daru said. “Today you can push.”

They lifted together. The wheel cleared the rut. On Tavo's bag, the little copper bird turned in the wind.

**The end.**
